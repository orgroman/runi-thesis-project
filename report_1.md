# Negation in Semantic Similarity Models for Patent Retrieval: A Literature Review

## Introduction  
Dense embedding models like BERT and Sentence-BERT (SBERT) have become central to semantic similarity and retrieval tasks, including patent prior-art search and legal case matching. These models convert text into high-dimensional vectors, allowing similarity to be measured by vector distance. However, a known limitation is their difficulty in handling logical constructs such as negation – the presence of words like “not” or other negation structures can invert sentence meaning, yet embedding models often fail to reflect this inversion ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Previous%20work%20has%20shown%20that,most%20important%20model%20selection%20criteria)). In domains like patents, which use formal logic-heavy language, this issue is particularly critical. For example, a patent claim may include a *negative limitation* (explicitly excluding an element), and ignoring the negation could cause one to wrongly equate an invention that *lacks* a component with one that requires it ([Duane Morris LLP - Silence Is Not Necessarily Golden for Negative Patent Claim Limitations](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/silence_is_not_necessarily_golden_negative_patent_claim_limitations_0722.html#:~:text=During%20the%20prosecution%20of%20a,Accord)). This review surveys prior research on how negation affects semantic similarity in dense embeddings, with an emphasis on the patent domain. We cover known challenges, evaluate domain-specific studies (and analogous work in the legal domain where relevant), identify datasets and tools for negation, and highlight approaches – including model enhancements and hybrid strategies – proposed to improve negation handling in semantic similarity tasks.

## Challenges of Negation in Dense Embeddings  
Negation fundamentally alters the meaning of a sentence, but dense vector models do not inherently handle this logical flip well. Classic distributional semantics tends to place antonyms (e.g. *hot* vs *cold*) or negated vs. affirmative statements in proximity because they share contextual words. In a simple embedding approach that averages word vectors, a sentence and its negation differ mainly by the vector for “not,” which by itself may not offset the other words’ influence ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=A%20lot%20of%20similarity%20methods,is%20usually%20used%20in%20NLP)). Even contextual transformers like BERT, which in principle can learn the role of negation, often underweight the “not.” Empirical probes have shown that BERT-based encoders sometimes treat “I like _X_” and “I do not like _X_” as very similar ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Previous%20work%20has%20shown%20that,most%20important%20model%20selection%20criteria)). For instance, one evaluation found that a universal sentence encoder gave a higher similarity score to a negated pair of sentences than to a truly equivalent pair with different wording ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=However%2C%20while%20these%20models%20generally,these%20opposite%20sentences)). This counter-intuitive result arises because the two negated sentences share most of their words in common, and the model fails to grasp the logical contradiction. In essence, **dense embeddings can overlook small function words or morphological cues that reverse meaning**. 

In response to this issue, researchers have constructed targeted benchmarks to diagnose it. Ettinger (2020) and others demonstrated that BERT’s internal representations do not adequately capture the effect of negation ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Previous%20work%20has%20shown%20that,most%20important%20model%20selection%20criteria)). Vahtola *et al.* (2022) introduced the **SemAntoNeg** test suite to specifically measure whether sentence embeddings treat negated-antonym pairs appropriately ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=paraphrasticity%20in%20sentences%20incorporating%20nega%02tion,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)). In SemAntoNeg, a model should recognize, for example, *“I am not guilty”* vs *“I am innocent”* as paraphrases with the same meaning, while differentiating them from *“I am guilty.”* Many general-purpose similarity models struggled on this test, confirming that **negation and antonymy remain challenging phenomena** for current embeddings ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=paraphrasticity%20in%20sentences%20incorporating%20nega%02tion,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)). In summary, the literature finds that off-the-shelf embeddings often *do not preserve logical meaning under negation*, which can lead to high similarity scores for sentences that are in fact semantically opposite ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Previous%20work%20has%20shown%20that,most%20important%20model%20selection%20criteria)) ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.nllp-1.24.pdf#:~:text=represent%20sentences%2C%20fail%20to%20capture,embedding%20models%20are%20applied%20directly)).

## Impact in Patent and Legal Domains  
The patent domain provides a real-world stress test for negation handling. Patent claims and specifications are rich in negation-like constructs – e.g. phrases like “*absent X*” or “*wherein no Y is present*” are used to define an invention’s scope ([Duane Morris LLP - Silence Is Not Necessarily Golden for Negative Patent Claim Limitations](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/silence_is_not_necessarily_golden_negative_patent_claim_limitations_0722.html#:~:text=During%20the%20prosecution%20of%20a,Accord)). Such subtle cues can completely change the legal meaning of a patent. If an embedding-based patent search system ignores a negation, it might judge an irrelevant patent (which has the opposite feature) as highly similar, leading to erroneous retrievals. Unfortunately, there is limited published research explicitly analyzing negation in patent embeddings. Nonetheless, several studies allude to related issues. For example, the **Patent Phrase Similarity** dataset introduced by Google in 2022 includes antonym pairs as a category to ensure models can identify when two patent phrases are opposites ([Announcing the Patent Phrase Similarity Dataset](https://research.google/blog/announcing-the-patent-phrase-similarity-dataset/#:~:text=contains%20,was%20used%20by%20Kaggle%20and)). This dataset was used in a competition to improve patent semantic search, and initial results showed that fine-tuned models perform much better than generic ones on technical phrases ([Announcing the Patent Phrase Similarity Dataset](https://research.google/blog/announcing-the-patent-phrase-similarity-dataset/#:~:text=meronym%2C%20and%20domain%20related,dataset%20perform%20substantially%20better%20than)). The inclusion of antonyms suggests that practitioners recognize the importance of capturing “opposite meaning” in the patent context.

Domain-adapted models like **PatentBERT** (a BERT trained on patent corpora) improve overall text understanding in patents, but do not inherently solve negation issues if not explicitly trained for it. Recent patent similarity approaches focus on technical nomenclature and context: for instance, Peng and Yang (2024) combine local BERT embeddings with a global citation graph to better match patent phrases ([](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-naacl.121.pdf#:~:text=prevailing%20semantic%20tex%02tual%20similarity%20approaches%2C,depth)). While effective for terminology, such methods do not specifically address negation. We can infer the gap by analogy to the legal domain, where more analysis exists. Legal texts (contracts, regulations, case law) similarly contain critical negations (e.g. “*shall not*”). Li *et al.* (2022) observed that when applying state-of-the-art sentence embeddings to legal clauses, *“small but relevant differences can be easily overlooked”* ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.nllp-1.24.pdf#:~:text=represent%20sentences%2C%20fail%20to%20capture,embedding%20models%20are%20applied%20directly)) – for example, negating a single phrase in an otherwise parallel contract clause might be ignored by a similarity model. They developed a model to explicitly detect relevant differences between nearly identical provisions, underlining that pure embeddings may overestimate similarity of legally contradictory statements ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.nllp-1.24.pdf#:~:text=similarity,text%20difference%20detection%20model%20which)) ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.nllp-1.24.pdf#:~:text=but%20relevant%20differences%20can%20be,body%20of%20research%20on%20Natural)). This finding likely extends to patents: an examiner comparing claims needs to catch a *“without ingredient Z”* clause, but a vanilla SBERT model might miss its significance. Thus, **in high-stakes domains like patents and law, embedding models’ insensitivity to negation is a serious concern**, and researchers are beginning to respond with specialized techniques.

## Strategies to Handle Negation in Semantic Similarity Models  
To address these limitations, several methodologies have been proposed. Broadly, they fall into two categories: (1) **enhancements to the embedding models or training regimes** to make them inherently more negation-aware, and (2) **hybrid or post-processing approaches** that incorporate logical rules or external knowledge to correct embedding outputs. Table 1 summarizes key research efforts in this area.

- **Fine-tuning on NLI/Entailment Tasks:** One straightforward approach is to leverage natural language inference (NLI) datasets, where models learn to detect *contradiction* versus *entailment*. Since negation often appears in contradiction examples, an NLI-trained model internalizes more logic. Indeed, Vahtola *et al.* (2022) found that models fine-tuned on NLI (e.g. MNLI) *outperform general-purpose embedding models* on negation-heavy similarity tests ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=paraphrasticity%20in%20sentences%20incorporating%20nega%02tion,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)). For example, an NLI-tuned DeBERTa or RoBERTa ranked at the top on the SemAntoNeg benchmark, whereas even sentence-transformers trained on STS or paraphrase data were “rather mediocre” on this negation task ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=things%2C%20we%20show%20that%20language,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)). Similarly, the winning ensembles in patent phrase similarity have incorporated NLI-aware models. Yu *et al.* (2024) boosted patent similarity by ensembling a PatentBERT with a DeBERTa model fine-tuned on MNLI and other inference tasks ([Semantic Similarity Matching for Patent Documents Using Ensemble BERT-related Model and Novel Text Processing Method](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.06782v1#:~:text=strengths%20of%20multiple%20BERT,advancing%20the%20field%20of%20CPC)). The NLI component adds a layer of logical understanding, helping catch cases where one patent phrase negates another. In short, *infusing entailment knowledge via fine-tuning is an effective strategy* to improve negation sensitivity in embeddings ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=paraphrasticity%20in%20sentences%20incorporating%20nega%02tion,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)).

- **Data Augmentation and Contrastive Learning:** Another line of work generates targeted training data to teach models about negation. Zhang *et al.* (2024) introduced a **triplet-based training** for intent embeddings that explicitly includes a negated variant of an utterance as a “hard negative” example ([Can Your Model Tell a Negation from an Implicature? Unravelling Challenges With Intent Encoders](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04314v1#:~:text=in%20real,For)) ([Can Your Model Tell a Negation from an Implicature? Unravelling Challenges With Intent Encoders](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04314v1#:~:text=interpret%20the%20semantic%20dimensions%20of,performance%20on%20the%20triplet%20task)). In their setup, the model must embed an original sentence closer to an implied similar meaning (implicature) than to its negated form. They *automatically generated negation* examples using an LLM (ChatGPT) and applied a contrastive loss to push apart embeddings of negated vs. affirmative sentences ([Can Your Model Tell a Negation from an Implicature? Unravelling Challenges With Intent Encoders](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04314v1#:~:text=negation%20and%20evaluates%20if%20the,loop%20quality%20control%20mechanisms)) ([Can Your Model Tell a Negation from an Implicature? Unravelling Challenges With Intent Encoders](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04314v1#:~:text=interpret%20the%20semantic%20dimensions%20of,performance%20on%20the%20triplet%20task)). This resulted in significant improvements: before, the model would embed negations too close to the original intent (treating *“don’t play music”* nearly like *“play music”*), but after training, it learned to separate them ([Can Your Model Tell a Negation from an Implicature? Unravelling Challenges With Intent Encoders](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04314v1#:~:text=that%20current%20representation%20of%20the,see%20Figure%202)) ([Can Your Model Tell a Negation from an Implicature? Unravelling Challenges With Intent Encoders](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04314v1#:~:text=,Figure%202%20shows%20that)). Contrastive learning on such augmented data has proven effective in making embeddings *reflect semantic differences rather than surface similarity*. Anschütz *et al.* (2023) similarly created the **CANNOT** dataset (Compilation of ANnotated Negation-Oriented Text-pairs) to fine-tune a sentence transformer specifically on negation distinctions ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=,see%20example%20below)). They generated thousands of sentence pairs where one is a negation of the other (using a rule-based negation tool) and trained a model (called **Neg-MPNet**) to reduce the similarity of negated pairs. The resulting model achieved much lower cosine similarity for negation pairs compared to the base model ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=%2A%20A%20rule,nlp%2FNegBLEURT)) ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=,don%27t%20make%20me%20feel%20relaxed)). For example, on a test pair “I like rainy days...” vs “I **don’t** like rainy days...”, the original SBERT gave an alarmingly high similarity (~0.93) while the negation-aware model dropped it to ~0.38, reflecting the true dissimilarity ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=,don%27t%20make%20me%20feel%20relaxed)). These works show that *targeted data and losses can teach an embedding model to treat negation as a meaningful change*, without losing performance on ordinary examples ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Large%20language%20models%20underestimate%20the,far%20while%20preserving%20their%20base)).

- **Modified Pre-training Objectives:** Rather than (or in addition to) fine-tuning, some researchers modify the language model during pre-training to encode logical negation. Hosseini *et al.* (2021) proposed **BERT\_NOT**, which augments BERT’s pre-training with an *unlikelihood* objective on negated sentences ([Understanding by Understanding Not: Modeling Negation in Language Models - ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.102/#:~:text=Negation%20is%20a%20core%20construction,on%20the%20negated%20NLI%20benchmarks)). In essence, they sample statements and automatically negate them, then train BERT such that the negated sentence is *less* likely (penalizing the model if it predicts the negated variant when it shouldn’t) ([Understanding by Understanding Not: Modeling Negation in Language Models - ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.102/#:~:text=Negation%20is%20a%20core%20construction,on%20the%20negated%20NLI%20benchmarks)). This teaches the model a sharper distinction between a sentence and its negation during the foundational training. BERT\_NOT achieved a dramatically lower error rate (only ~4%) on a LAMA knowledge probe for negated facts, versus much higher error for vanilla BERT ([Understanding by Understanding Not: Modeling Negation in Language Models - ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.102/#:~:text=Negation%20is%20a%20core%20construction,on%20the%20negated%20NLI%20benchmarks)). It also improved performance on negation-specific NLI benchmarks ([Understanding by Understanding Not: Modeling Negation in Language Models - ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.102/#:~:text=Negation%20is%20a%20core%20construction,on%20the%20negated%20NLI%20benchmarks)). Such pre-training enhancements effectively imbue the embedding space with a built-in sensitivity to negation, making downstream similarity comparisons more reliable on logically negated inputs. Another example is the work of Moore and Barnes (2021), who in a sentiment analysis context used **multi-task learning**: they added an auxiliary objective for negation and speculation detection while training a classifier, which in turn helped the model handle negated sentiment phrases better ([F 1 -s results for the negation test split, where the values represent...](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/F-1-s-results-for-the-negation-test-split-where-the-values-represent-the-mean-standard_tbl3_352365971#:~:text=represent,)). While that was a classification task, the principle can extend to embedding learning – multi-task frameworks can incorporate a negation-focused task (like detecting if a sentence has a negation or not) to guide the model to encode that information.

- **Hybrid Rule-Based + Neural Methods:** In mission-critical domains, a purely neural approach can be supplemented with rule-based logic to catch negation. One simple hybrid strategy is to perform **negation detection** as a preprocessing step (using tools like *negSpaCy* for medical text, which identifies negated terms ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=There%20are%20ways%20to%20work,doesn%27t%20help%20with%20similarity%20scores))) and then adjust the similarity computation accordingly. For instance, if two patent claims are identical except that one contains a negation like “without ingredient A”, a rule-based system could flag this and either reduce the similarity score or treat the pair as a special case. While few published patent retrieval systems explicitly document such rules, the idea is frequently suggested in Q&A forums and practical discussions ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=way%20the%20word%20,usually%20used%20in%20NLP)). The challenge is determining *how* to integrate the rule output – one approach is a cascade: use the embedding model for broad retrieval, then an **entailment model** to filter out results that contradict the query. In other words, after using embeddings to find candidate patents, an NLI model can verify if a candidate logically entails or contradicts the query description (negation being a prime cause of contradiction) ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=If%20you%20want%20to%20evaluate,determined%20embeddings%20will%20not%20suffice)) ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=You%20might%20have%20some%20luck,no%20bearing%20on%20another%20statement)). If a contradiction is found (e.g. the query asks for an apparatus *without X* but the candidate patent requires X), the system can discard or down-rank that result. This two-step hybrid (semantic retrieval + logic filter) is analogous to approaches in the legal domain where retrieval is followed by textual entailment checks in tasks like the COLIEE competition. Another hybrid approach is to incorporate external knowledge bases: e.g., using **lexicons of antonyms** or structured ontologies. Some researchers have retrofitted word embeddings using lexical constraints so that antonymous words repel each other in the vector space ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Previous%20work%20has%20shown%20that,most%20important%20model%20selection%20criteria)). For contextual models, one could imagine integrating WordNet antonym pairs or patent-specific thesauri during fine-tuning – ensuring that pairs like “enabled” vs “not enabled” are encoded distinctly. A recent framework, **SUMEX**, combined an ontology with a transformer model to both compute similarity and generate explanations ([SUMEX: A hybrid framework for Semantic textUal siMilarity and ...](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306457324001316#:~:text=SUMEX%3A%20A%20hybrid%20framework%20for,art)). While SUMEX was not solely about negation, such an approach could be extended: the ontology could encode that a certain legal or technical phrase is the negation/absence of another, and the model could then treat that as a significant semantic difference. Overall, hybrid strategies are still emerging, but they represent a promising avenue to inject logical **precision** into otherwise fuzzy semantic embeddings.

## Datasets and Benchmarks for Negation in Text Similarity  
Research in this area has been accelerated by new datasets that expose models to negation phenomena or evaluate their handling of negation. We highlight a few relevant datasets and benchmarks (see Table 2):

- **SemAntoNeg (2022):** A focused evaluation set of 3,152 sentence pairs designed to probe negation and antonymy in paraphrase identification ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=on%20dedicated%20test%20suites%20that,that%20include%20combinations%20of%20negated)) ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=a%20simple%20example%2C%20a%20semantic,for%20a%20model%20to%20succeed)). Each entry consists of an original sentence and three candidates, among which models must pick the true paraphrase. The distractors include tricky variants like a negated sentence or an antonym substitution. This benchmark is *not* for training, but for testing a model’s understanding of negation’s effect on meaning. It revealed large variation in model performance and has become a diagnostic tool for sentence embeddings ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=paraphrasticity%20in%20sentences%20incorporating%20nega%02tion,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)).

- **Patent Phrase Similarity dataset (2022):** A large dataset (~50k phrase pairs) of human-rated similarities in context of patent classification ([Announcing the Patent Phrase Similarity Dataset](https://research.google/blog/announcing-the-patent-phrase-similarity-dataset/#:~:text=Today%2C%20we%20announce%20the%20release,rating%20classes%20similar%20to%20WordNet)). Importantly, it provides fine-grained labels beyond just a score – pairs are categorized as *synonym, antonym, hypernym/hyponym, holonym/meronym,* or *unrelated*, etc. ([Announcing the Patent Phrase Similarity Dataset](https://research.google/blog/announcing-the-patent-phrase-similarity-dataset/#:~:text=focuses%20on%20technical%20terms%20from,0%20International)). The inclusion of **antonym** pairs means models evaluated on this set must assign low similarity to pairs that are opposites in meaning. For example, a patent phrase “includes component A” versus “devoid of component A” would be labeled antonyms (or semantically dissimilar). This dataset, released via a Kaggle competition ([Announcing the Patent Phrase Similarity Dataset](https://research.google/blog/announcing-the-patent-phrase-similarity-dataset/#:~:text=meronym%2C%20and%20domain%20related,Initial%20results%20show)), has driven development of patent-specific models. It serves as both a training resource (to fine-tune models on patent semantics) and an evaluation benchmark for how well models capture similarity and contrast in technical terms.

- **CANNOT Negation Dataset (2023):** A compilation of negation-oriented text pairs introduced by Anschütz *et al.* Specifically created to improve evaluation metrics, it comprises pairs of sentences where one is the negation of the other (collected and generated from various sources) ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=The%20following%20artifacts%20were%20released,a%20result%20from%20our%20work)). The dataset is available publicly ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=,compared%20to%20their%20base%20models)) and has been used to fine-tune sentence transformers and even to create a negation-aware version of the BLEURT metric. CANNOT is useful for *training* models (teaching them negation) and for *probing* – e.g., one can measure if a metric like BERTScore correctly drops when evaluating a negated hypothesis vs a reference. While not domain-specific, its methodology (rule-based negation of real sentences) could be applied to patent text: one could create negated patent sentences to test a patent model’s sensitivity.

- **NLI and Entailment Corpora:** Datasets such as **MultiNLI (Williams et al., 2018)** and the **Stanford NLI (SNLI)** have provided indirectly relevant training data. They contain many instances of negation in the *contradiction* examples (e.g., premise: “Alice likes dogs.” hypothesis: “Alice does **not** like dogs.” labeled contradiction). These corpora have been widely used to fine-tune transformer models. Although not created solely for negation, they are a key resource – as seen by the success of NLI-fine-tuned models on SemAntoNeg ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=things%2C%20we%20show%20that%20language,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)). Additionally, specialized test sets like the **Negation NLI dataset** from Hosseni *et al.* (as mentioned in BERT_NOT research) evaluate how well models handle negated entailments ([Understanding by Understanding Not: Modeling Negation in Language Models - ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.102/#:~:text=Negation%20is%20a%20core%20construction,on%20the%20negated%20NLI%20benchmarks)). 

- **Legal and Biomedical Negation Datasets:** In the legal domain, there are entailment datasets (e.g., COLIEE’s yes/no question answering from statutes) that require understanding negation in laws, though these are not framed as similarity tasks. In biomedical NLP, corpora like **BioScope** and others annotate negation scopes in clinical text, but those are for detection tasks rather than semantic similarity. Still, a negation detection tool from biomedicine (like negSpaCy’s models) can be repurposed to help preprocess data in similarity tasks ([python - Sentence similarity models not capturing opposite sentences - Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/69374258/sentence-similarity-models-not-capturing-opposite-sentences#:~:text=There%20are%20ways%20to%20work,doesn%27t%20help%20with%20similarity%20scores)).

## Conclusion  
Negation remains a challenging aspect of semantic similarity for dense embedding models, but research over the past few years has made significant strides in pinpointing and addressing this limitation. **In summary, standard BERT/SBERT embeddings often do not fully grasp negation, leading to overly high similarity scores for semantically opposite texts** ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Previous%20work%20has%20shown%20that,most%20important%20model%20selection%20criteria)). This can be especially problematic in domains like patents and legal documents, where a single negation can entail a critical difference in meaning ([Duane Morris LLP - Silence Is Not Necessarily Golden for Negative Patent Claim Limitations](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/silence_is_not_necessarily_golden_negative_patent_claim_limitations_0722.html#:~:text=During%20the%20prosecution%20of%20a,Accord)). Prior studies have highlighted the issue and paved the way with new benchmarks such as SemAntoNeg (for general language) and specialized datasets in the patent domain that include antonyms ([Announcing the Patent Phrase Similarity Dataset](https://research.google/blog/announcing-the-patent-phrase-similarity-dataset/#:~:text=contains%20,was%20used%20by%20Kaggle%20and)).

To improve embedding models, researchers have explored a spectrum of solutions. Fine-tuning on NLI tasks or using entailment models injects logical reasoning capability, yielding embeddings that better respect negation (as evidenced by NLI-tuned models excelling on negation tests) ([](https://aclanthology.org/2022.blackboxnlp-1.20.pdf#:~:text=paraphrasticity%20in%20sentences%20incorporating%20nega%02tion,rather%20mediocre%20in%20our%20task)). Task-specific fine-tuning with generated negation examples, as well as novel training objectives like unlikelihood loss for negation ([Understanding by Understanding Not: Modeling Negation in Language Models - ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.naacl-main.102/#:~:text=Negation%20is%20a%20core%20construction,on%20the%20negated%20NLI%20benchmarks)), have proven effective at reducing negation-related errors. These model-centric approaches are complemented by hybrid strategies: combining neural embeddings with rule-based negation detectors or symbolic knowledge can catch cases the embeddings miss, thereby improving reliability in applications. In the patent retrieval context, one can envision integrated systems that use embeddings for initial search but then apply a logical filter to ensure, for example, that if a query says “excluding component X,” the retrieved patents do not all contain *X*. Early implementations of such ideas (e.g., ensembles mixing a patent-trained SBERT with an NLI model ([Semantic Similarity Matching for Patent Documents Using Ensemble BERT-related Model and Novel Text Processing Method](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.06782v1#:~:text=strengths%20of%20multiple%20BERT,advancing%20the%20field%20of%20CPC))) show promise in boosting accuracy on challenging cases.

Looking ahead, **negation-aware semantic models are likely to become standard practice** in domains requiring precise understanding. The development of negation-focused resources (like the CANNOT dataset and negation-aware metrics ([[2307.13989] This is not correct! Negation-aware Evaluation of Language Generation Systems](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2307.13989#:~:text=Large%20language%20models%20underestimate%20the,far%20while%20preserving%20their%20base))) is facilitating this evolution by allowing researchers to train and rigorously evaluate models on logical consistency. In the patent domain, there is room to create tailored negation benchmarks – for instance, taking claim sentences and perturbing them with negations – to ensure patent search models can distinguish presence vs. absence of features. Bridging the gap between informal language understanding and formal logical reasoning remains a frontier. Hybrid neuro-symbolic approaches, which marry data-driven embeddings with the exactness of rule-based logic, are an exciting avenue to explore for patent and legal NLP. By combining the strengths of both, we can achieve semantic similarity systems that are not only fluent and generalizable, but also **attuned to the nuances of negation and other logical constructs**, thereby improving the precision of patent retrieval and similar applications.
